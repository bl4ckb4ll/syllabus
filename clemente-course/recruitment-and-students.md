# Recruitment and students in the first Clemente Course

The Clemente experiment did not begin by enrolling ordinary tuition-paying college students. Earl Shorris deliberately tried to recruit adults living in poverty and then remove some of the immediate practical reasons they could not attend a demanding evening course.

Primary account:
https://harpers.org/archive/1997/09/ii-as-a-weapon-in-the-hands-of-the-restless-poor/

## Where the idea came from

While researching poverty, Shorris interviewed incarcerated women at **Bedford Hills Correctional Facility**. A conversation with Viniece "Niecie" Walker was decisive. Walker argued that poor people were excluded from the kind of reflective cultural life available "downtown" — museums, lectures, plays, concerts, and the humanities. Shorris turned that claim into an experiment: teach a serious humanities course and see what happened.

Dr. Jaime Inclán offered a conference room at the **Roberto Clemente Family Guidance Center** in lower Manhattan, which became the first classroom.

## Original selection criteria

Shorris listed four basic criteria:

- **Age:** 18–35.
- **Household income:** below 150% of the Census Bureau's official poverty threshold, with some later adjustment.
- **Reading:** able to read a tabloid newspaper.
- **Intent:** expressed an intention to complete the course.

The point was not to screen for conventional academic prestige. Shorris did reject applicants whose household resources put them outside the poverty criterion, applicants who could not yet read at the minimum level needed for the course, and people whose schedules made attendance impossible.

## Recruiting organizations and sites

Shorris and the Clemente Center worked through organizations already serving poor and working-class New Yorkers, including:

- **Roberto Clemente Family Guidance Center**;
- **Neighborhood Youth & Family Services**, South Bronx;
- **The Door**;
- **Grand Street Settlement**;
- **Local 1199** / hospital-worker contacts;
- other social-service and youth programs around New York City.

About fifty candidates were considered for thirty places in the first class.

## The failed first pitch

At an early South Bronx recruiting meeting, a dispute broke out over whether the course should teach African history rather than the proposed American-history component. Shorris defended the planned curriculum badly and **none of that group applied**.

That failure mattered. At the next recruitment session, at **The Door**, he changed the presentation. Rather than talking abstractly about an educational experiment, he explained exactly what students would receive and exactly how demanding the work would be.

## The successful pitch

The substance of the revised pitch was:

- transportation paid;
- childcare provided when needed;
- food/snack provided;
- books and course materials provided;
- college-level work comparable in seriousness to a first-year humanities course at an elite university;
- attendance expected through bad weather and ordinary difficulties;
- tests and papers required;
- initially, no promise of college credit or economic reward;
- the immediate offer was the humanities themselves: **philosophy, poetry, art history, logic, rhetoric, and American history**.

All but one of the prospective students at that Door presentation applied. Shorris then repeated the revised presentation elsewhere.

## Who actually entered

The admitted group was heterogeneous. Shorris's account includes people who had experienced homelessness, incarceration, addiction/recovery, pregnancy, unstable work, severe poverty, immigration and difficulty gaining access to conventional schools. Some were working low-wage jobs; some were relying on public or charitable support. A few immigrants with imperfect English were admitted despite Shorris's initial assumption that the course would not include them.

This matters to the history of the project: Clemente was not merely a cheap evening Great Books class. Shorris was testing a claim about **people living inside what he called the "surround of force" of poverty**.

## Material support

The course tried to remove small but decisive barriers rather than treating them as evidence of insufficient commitment:

- subway/bus tokens;
- childcare;
- dinner, snack, or sandwich;
- books and other course materials;
- free instruction from the faculty.

Classes were scheduled in the evening, roughly 6:00–7:30 PM.

## Completion and credit

Of thirty admitted students in the first year, sixteen completed the course and fourteen earned Bard College credit. Shorris initially could promise only a certificate; Bard's involvement turned the experiment into credit-bearing college work.

The Clemente organization's own retrospective history says the first class graduated in spring 1996 at the Roberto Clemente Family Guidance Center:
https://www.clementecourse.org/history

## Related prison course

The first course was videotaped in part as documentation. A related Clemente version also operated at Bedford Hills Correctional Facility, where Walker later served as Shorris's teaching assistant. Keep the prison version distinct from the thirty-person Manhattan first cohort when counting enrollment or completion.
