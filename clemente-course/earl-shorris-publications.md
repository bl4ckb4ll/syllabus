# Earl Shorris: Clemente publication trail

This file answers two separate questions: **where did Shorris publicly explain the Clemente experiment?** and **which texts are retrospective books rather than contemporary reports?**

## Contemporary / near-contemporary accounts

### 1997 — *New American Blues*

Earl Shorris, *New American Blues: A Journey Through Poverty to Democracy*. New York: W. W. Norton, 1997.

This was the poverty book Shorris was already researching when the Clemente idea emerged. The relevant material includes the chapters **"Radical Humanism"** and **"The Clemente Experiment"**, plus **Appendix C**, which contains Jaime Inclán's evaluation material. It is a major source for the recruitment process, teachers, rotation of subjects, classroom episodes, assignments, and Shorris's theory of the "surround of force."

### September 1997 — *Harper's Magazine*

Earl Shorris, **"On the Uses of a Liberal Education: II. As a Weapon in the Hands of the Restless Poor."** *Harper's Magazine*, September 1997.

https://harpers.org/archive/1997/09/ii-as-a-weapon-in-the-hands-of-the-restless-poor/

This is the most useful short contemporary narrative of the first two years. It gives the origin at Bedford Hills, the first teachers, selection criteria, failed and successful recruiting meetings, material support, the philosophy syllabus, the first assignment, completion numbers, and Shorris's political interpretation of the experiment.

Harper's describes Shorris as a regular contributor to the magazine from 1972 until his death in 2012 and identifies this essay as appearing in the September 1997 issue.

### Winter 1997–98 — *American Educator*

Earl Shorris, **"The Triumphant Power of the Humanities."** *American Educator* 21, no. 4 (Winter 1997–98), pp. 2–7, 43–45.

ERIC record:
https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ559054

ERIC classifies it as a journal article describing the development and implementation of the pilot humanities program for people living in poverty.

## Full-length retrospective account

### 2000 — *Riches for the Poor*

Earl Shorris, *Riches for the Poor: The Clemente Course in the Humanities*. New York: W. W. Norton, 2000.

This is the book devoted specifically to Clemente. Its scope is broader than the first New York class: it develops the political theory behind the course, recounts the experiment and curriculum, discusses criticism and variations, and follows Clemente into other places and cultures.

Useful chapter/topic sequence includes:

- definitions of poverty and politics;
- the "surround of force";
- work and citizenship;
- the Bedford Hills / prison epiphany;
- radical humanism;
- the Clemente experiment;
- Bard College and credit;
- curriculum;
- variations and self-criticism;
- other countries and cultures;
- the political consequences of educating the poor.

## Later reflection

### 2013 — *The Art of Freedom*

Earl Shorris, *The Art of Freedom: Teaching the Humanities to the Poor*. New York: W. W. Norton, 2013. Published posthumously.

This is a late retrospective treatment of the project and its spread, not evidence that every reading mentioned there belonged to the original 1995–96 syllabus.

## Interviews / conversations worth preserving separately

### ca. 2000 — Oklahoma Humanities Council conversation

A published conversation/interview with Shorris and J. Thomas May, commonly titled **"The Humanities as an Antidote to Poverty?"**, contains retrospective discussion of Clemente and Shorris's curriculum. It is useful corroboration but should not outrank the detailed 1997 account when the two differ.

One important example: Shorris retrospectively mentioned **Hume** in recounting the philosophy curriculum, but the exact Hume work is not identified and Hume is absent from his detailed pre-course syllabus as reported in 1997. This repository therefore labels Hume **RETROSPECTIVE**.

### 2013 — Timothy Koranda letter in *Harper's*

After Shorris's death, original logic instructor Timothy Koranda described his role in a letter to *Harper's*. He recalled being asked to use mathematical logic to teach abstraction, beginning with Aristotle and proceeding to propositional and first-order predicate logic.

https://harpers.org/archive/2013/11/letters-814/

This is valuable for reconstructing the logic course but is retrospective testimony, not a surviving 1995 syllabus.

## Related but not automatically part of the Clemente bibliography

Shorris wrote widely for *Harper's* and elsewhere about poverty, politics, democracy, education, and American public life. Do not tag every Shorris essay as "Clemente" merely because it shares those concerns. The files above privilege publications that actually describe, evaluate, or retrospectively reconstruct the course.
