# Imre Kertész — “Who Owns Auschwitz?”

Essay on Holocaust memory, representation, appropriation, and what happens when survivors' lived memory passes into public culture.

- **Author:** Imre Kertész
- **English translation:** John MacKay
- **Publication:** *The Yale Journal of Criticism* 14, no. 1 (2001), pp. 267–272
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1353/yale.2001.0010
- **Readable PDF:** https://waukeshatemple.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/104/2020/10/Kertesz-Who-Owns-Auschwitz.pdf

## Why it belongs

Kertész begins from the problem that Auschwitz cannot remain the exclusive possession of its survivors as they age and die. The essay asks what happens when direct memory becomes inherited cultural memory: who may represent it, how it can be falsified or appropriated, and what is lost when historical catastrophe is converted into a familiar public ritual or consumable story.

A central concern is **stylization and kitsch**. Kertész argues that mass cultural recognition of the Holocaust came with a danger: representation can turn catastrophe into sentimental reassurance, moral spectacle, or a story whose conventional emotional shape leaves the audience's underlying world intact. His criticism of *Schindler's List* is part of this larger argument rather than a complaint that art must not represent Auschwitz.

For Black Ball, the essay belongs with questions about:

- survivor memory versus inherited collective memory;
- who claims authority over historical trauma;
- artistic representation versus commemoration and ritual;
- kitsch, sentimental closure, and commercial mass culture;
- appropriation, falsification, and institutionalized public memory;
- whether representations of atrocity preserve or neutralize their ethical consequences.
