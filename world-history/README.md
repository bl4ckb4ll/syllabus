# World history / common-knowledge gaps

This is an exploratory source map for widening the bookshelf beyond a compressed US/Western-Europe canon. It is **not** a claim that any one school, degree, country, or graduate reads only that canon, and it is not yet a complete world-history course.

The useful rule here is: connect a place or historical problem to an inspectable course, reading list, primary text, or serious secondary source. Podcasts can be good entry points, but they should stay labeled as podcasts rather than silently becoming primary evidence.

Access status below was checked 2026-09-02.

## Free university-course anchors

### Ukraine and Eastern Europe

- **Timothy Snyder — _The Making of Modern Ukraine_ — Yale University, Fall 2022.** Yale makes the lectures free to the public and links the course syllabus and reading list. This is a strong first anchor for Ukraine while also opening Poland-Lithuania, the Russian Empire, Habsburg rule, the Soviet period, the Holocaust, and post-Soviet history.
  - https://online.yale.edu/courses/making-modern-ukraine

### Hebrew Bible / Old Testament

- **Christine Hayes — _Introduction to the Old Testament (Hebrew Bible)_ — Open Yale Courses, Fall 2006.** Twenty-four lectures plus syllabus, transcript/audio/video, and readings. The course explicitly places the biblical corpus in its Ancient Near Eastern historical and cultural setting and uses historical-critical, literary, source, redaction, and canonical approaches.
  - https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145

### New Testament and early Christianity

- **Dale B. Martin — _Introduction to the New Testament History and Literature_ — Open Yale Courses, Spring 2009.** Twenty-six lectures plus syllabus and transcripts. The course treats New Testament and early-Christian documents as historical sources and ancient literature; lecture 13 is specifically **The Historical Jesus**.
  - https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-152

### Historical Jesus

- **Thomas Sheehan — _The Historical Jesus_ — Stanford Continuing Studies, 2007.** A surviving public distribution is indexed as an 11-part Stanford Continuing Studies audio course, including a syllabus and lectures on Jesus, first-century Judaism, the Gospels, Paul, crucifixion, resurrection, and the first century of Christianity. The surviving listing says the series was released under CC BY-NC-ND.
  - https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/historical-jesus/id384233911

These Bible/Christian-history sources are useful precisely because a learner does not have to enroll in a degree or accept a confessional position to encounter serious historical study. A later church-history cluster should keep **historical scholarship**, **primary Christian texts**, and **confessional/theological teaching** visibly distinct rather than treating one as a substitute for the others.

## African kingdoms: ready-made British A-level scaffold

OCR's current A Level History A specification contains **African kingdoms c.1400–c.1800: four case studies** as a non-British history option. The same specification also contains **The rise and decline of the Mughal Empire in India 1526–1739**.

- OCR specification at a glance:
  - https://www.ocr.org.uk/qualifications/as-and-a-level/history-a-h105-h505-from-2015/specification-at-a-glance/
- OCR African Kingdoms teaching/support page:
  - https://www.ocr.org.uk/qualifications/as-and-a-level/history-a-h105-h505-from-2015/planning-and-teaching/
- **Toby Green — _African Kingdoms: A Guide to the Kingdoms of Songhay, Kongo, Benin, Oyo and Dahomey c.1400–c.1800_** — free OCR course e-book:
  - https://www.ocr.org.uk/Images/208299-african-kingdoms-ebook-.pdf
- King's College London-hosted teaching-resource site, with a Key Stage 5 scheme of work, webinars, and kingdom-by-kingdom material:
  - https://africankingdoms.co.uk/

This is unusually useful syllabus material because it is not just a general recommendation to “learn African history”: it supplies an actual examinable course structure, teacher resources, and a free course book. The resource set covers Benin, Dahomey, Kongo, Oyo, and Songhay; the wider site also points toward Mali, Asante, and further reading.

### Expansion queue beyond the OCR West-African cluster

Do not let “African kingdoms” collapse into only one region. Add sourced clusters for at least:

- Aksum / Ethiopia and the Horn;
- Great Zimbabwe / Mutapa and southern Africa;
- Kongo / Angola and west-central Africa;
- Great Lakes states;
- Swahili coast and Indian Ocean worlds;
- Sahelian states beyond a single Mali–Songhay narrative.

## South Asia / Mughal entry points

### Primary source

- **Zahir-ud-din Muhammad Babur — _Baburnama_ / _Memoirs of Babur_.** Open Library identifies a scan-backed edition linked to Internet Archive. Keep the translation/edition visible when citing passages; do not treat different English translations as identical.
  - https://openlibrary.org/books/OL1111677M/The_Baburnama

### Course scaffold

- OCR's A Level History A specification includes **The rise and decline of the Mughal Empire in India 1526–1739**, making it another concrete school-level route into a history that is often nearly absent from US survey courses.
  - https://www.ocr.org.uk/qualifications/as-and-a-level/history-a-h105-h505-from-2015/specification-at-a-glance/

### Podcast supplement — not a Mughal source

- **Fall of Civilizations, episode 14 — _Vijayanagara: The Last Emperors of South India_.** This is a useful long-form introduction to a major South Indian state and its wider world, but it should **not** be mislabeled as a Mughal episode.
  - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nlMpxUYKTcU

Fall of Civilizations is worth mining more broadly as a discovery layer — its corpus includes, among others, Songhai, Byzantium, Vijayanagara, Persia, Majapahit, and other societies — but each episode should lead onward to its cited scholarship and primary sources.

## Reconstruction / Sea Islands: overlap with Blackball

- **Eric Foner — _Reconstruction: America's Unfinished Revolution, 1863–1877_ (1988).** Keep the exact title. Particularly relevant early sections include **“Rehearsals for Reconstruction,” “The Meaning of Freedom,”** and **“Ambiguities of Free Labor.”**
  - ISBN 978-0-06-093716-4 (widely reprinted)
- **National Park Service — “The Port Royal Experiment.”** After the 1861 Battle of Port Royal, thousands of formerly enslaved people remained in control of the Sea Islands area while slaveholders fled. NPS emphasizes freedpeople as active participants who pressed for labor reform, land redistribution, education, and military service.
  - https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/the-port-royal-experiment.htm

### Next Sea Islands mining pass

Use Foner's notes/bibliography and the NPS source trail to recover the underlying scholarship on:

- Port Royal and the South Carolina Sea Islands;
- freedpeople's subsistence choices versus cotton production and wage/free-labor schemes;
- land ownership and redistribution;
- schools, churches, military service, and local political institutions;
- the conflict among freedpeople's own goals, Northern reformers, federal officials, and returning/former planters.

This is exactly the kind of material that belongs in both repositories: **syllabus** because it enlarges what someone can actually learn, and **Blackball** because it gives evidence for questions about labor, property, freedom, schooling, and whose definition of “progress” gets recorded.

## Geographic retrieval queue: places that should not stay blank

The next passes should attach at least one serious survey/lecture source and one primary-source or document collection where feasible to each cluster:

- **Moldova / Bessarabia / Transnistria** — Romanian, Ottoman, Russian, Soviet, and post-Soviet contexts;
- **Caucasus** — Armenia, Georgia, Azerbaijan, and the Ottoman/Persian/Russian borderlands;
- **Slovenia / Croatia / Dalmatia** — Habsburg, Venetian, Ottoman-border, Yugoslav, and post-Yugoslav contexts;
- **Bosnia / Serbia / Montenegro / Kosovo / North Macedonia / Albania / Bulgaria** — avoid flattening “the Balkans” into a single national story;
- **Black Sea worlds** — Ukraine, Crimea, Ottoman domains, steppe polities, Russia, Romania, Bulgaria, and trade/migration connections.

The purpose is not to manufacture a new list of Great Men. Prefer sources that expose **states, households, religion, language, trade, migration, labor, war, ordinary life, and competing historical memories**, then attach individuals to those structures where they actually illuminate them.

## Possible learner-facing source packs

A focused chatbot or guide can be useful when its corpus and boundaries are visible. Good early packs would be:

1. **Bible and early Christianity** — Hayes + Martin + Sheehan + primary texts, with historical and confessional claims separated.
2. **Ukraine / Black Sea / Eastern Europe** — Snyder as an anchor, then region-specific primary and secondary sources.
3. **African kingdoms** — OCR/Toby Green scaffold plus primary sources and scholarship beyond the OCR cases.
4. **South Asia** — Baburnama + Mughal course scaffold + Vijayanagara and other regional histories.
5. **Reconstruction / Sea Islands** — Foner + Port Royal primary/institutional sources + the scholarship Foner cites.

The learner should be able to ask “where did this claim come from?” and get a source, not merely another generated paragraph.
