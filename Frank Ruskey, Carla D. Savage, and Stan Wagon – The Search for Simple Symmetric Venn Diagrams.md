## Why this belongs in the favorites list

This is a particularly good example of a familiar elementary picture opening directly into real mathematics. An `n`-Venn diagram is not merely a collection of overlapping blobs: the curves are simple closed Jordan curves, they meet only finitely many times, and every one of the `2^n` possible inside/outside membership patterns must occur as a nonempty connected region.

That immediately separates several questions that ordinary textbook pictures blur together. Circles cannot give a Venn diagram for four or more sets: two circles meet in at most two points, and the resulting planar graph does not have enough regions. Arbitrary Jordan curves, however, are not subject to a corresponding finite upper bound.

The striking extra condition is rotational symmetry. For an `n`-fold rotationally symmetric `n`-Venn diagram, `n` must be prime. The paper connects this geometric constraint with binomial coefficients, the Boolean lattice, symmetric-chain decompositions, planar topology, and explicit constructions. The necessary prime condition goes back to David Henderson; the sufficiency result was proved much later by Jerrold Griggs, Charles Killian, and Carla Savage.

The 2008 Wagon–Webb note is worth reading immediately afterward. It points out a gap in the traditional presentation of Henderson's argument and supplies the missing Jordan-curve step. That makes the pair especially attractive pedagogically: a seductive short argument survives, but only after a genuinely topological point is made precise.

Despite Stan Wagon's association with hyperbolic geometry elsewhere, hyperbolic geometry is not the engine of this result. The geometry here is planar, with Jordan curves and rotations; the other main ingredients are combinatorial.
