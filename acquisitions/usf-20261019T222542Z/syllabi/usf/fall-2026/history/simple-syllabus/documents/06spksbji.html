University Course Description

A study of ancient Greece focusing on the brilliant period following the Persian Wars, but embracing as well the formative Bronze, Middle and Archaic ages, and the decline culminating in the conquest of Greece by Philip II of Macedon in 338 B.C.

Student Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

  • Trace the broad history of Ancient Greece.
  • Identify the key political, social, economic, and religious innovations of Classical antiquity.
  • Comprehend how many of these political and cultural innovations shifted over time. 
  • Utilize primary and secondary sources to develop and defend a critical argument about the past.

Course Objectives

Students will:

  1. Learn how to synthesize and engage with primary and secondary sources for the study of Ancient Greek History through weekly low-stakes assignments.
  2. Write two argumentative essays within the sub-field of ancient history that develop an original thesis.
  3. Be able to recall from memory the key characters, topics, and events that occurred in ancient Greek history before Alexander III the Great's campaigns in the 330s.

Grade Categories and Weights

  • Attendance & Participation (20%)
    • Attendance is mandatory. Please notify me by email before class if you need an excused absence. Students with excused absences will not be disadvantaged, but they must attend office hours to make up missed lecture notes.
    • Participation includes bringing questions about our readings to class, asking questions during lectures, and being respectful to whoever is talking (both to the instructor and to other students). This includes body language! If you are working with a computer, please be sure to look up from your screen regularly. Students who spend the majority of lecture looking at their screens and not at the board or the speakers will lose participation points.
  • Weekly Reading Quizzes (10%)
    • There will be reading quizzes at the start of every class, but only 50% of them (one per week) will count towards your quiz score. The other quiz, called the Check-In, is meant to help you prepare for the exam and maintain a direct line of communication with the professor. Each quiz will consist of one question.
  • Low-Stakes Assignments (15%)
    • At regular intervals throughout the semester, I will assign low-stakes assignments that must be completed on Canvas. These will be announced in advance, outlined and discussed in lecture, and due on Friday evenings. 
    • The first assignment is to visit office hours.
  • Paper 1: Primary Source Paper (10%)
    • A 1000-word essay is due at the end of week nine. We will work through potential topics, thesis statements, and the paper structure in class.
  • Paper 2: Secondary Source Paper (15%)
    • A 1500-word essay is due at the end of the regular lectures. I plan to distribute prompts at the start of week thirteen. 
    • Please notify me before the submission if you would like critical feedback. 
  • Final Exam (30%)
    • The final exam will be a memorization exam evaluating students’ mastery of the names, dates, places, and important themes of the course.
    • I will keep a running list of key terms on the board during lectures, and the exam will test students’ knowledge of these terms. A Key Terms list will be distributed to students on the final day of lecture.
    • The exam will be structured in two parts:
      • Multiple choice IDs for maps.
      • Fill-in-the-blank statements for important names, dates, political groups, and places. There will be a word bank for these sections.

Course Policies: Grades

Students should arrive to class on time and use their computers and devices appropriately. If you have a question about course material, please ask the instructor – chances are other students have the same question.

Students are expected to use non-racist and non-sexist language. Historians often study people and authors who use racially charged or discriminatory language, so it is important that we repeat historical terms and phrases sensitively and with respect and consideration to the victims of the language of the time and our peers in the present. In nearly every case, it is better to use modern terms than risk being insensitive. Please be respectful to each other.

Every student must produce their own work based on their own ideas and cite anyone else’s ideas or words appropriately. If in doubt, always provide a citation! You cannot overcite, and plagiarism, even if unintentional, may lead to serious academic consequences, including dismissal. Expectations on student ethics and integrity are available online: https://www.usf.edu/undergrad/students/ethics-integrity.aspx

Due to the ongoing legal cases over the training data for most LLMs, copying and pasting AI responses qualifies as plagiarism. There is a zero-tolerance policy on plagiarism at USF, and I am required to report any and all cases of suspected plagiarism immediately. Do your own work – I am always amenable to giving extensions, for any reason, so send me an email if you are feeling overwhelmed and need extra time.

You are required to submit a cover letter on every written paper acknowledging how you have engaged with AI. This cover letter does not count towards your word count. You will not be penalized for using AI for revision (this remains a contested point in the Academy – some journals will automatically reject papers that use AI for revision while others are encouraging it), but I will grade you appropriately. Both papers are meant to assess your ability to think critically about primary and secondary sources.

All written submissions should be in formal academic prose. Everyone is strongly encouraged to visit the Writing Studio within the Academic Success Center. Volunteer tutors are available to assist you both synchronously and asynchronously. For more information, visit https://www.usf.edu/undergrad/academic-success-center/writing-studio/ 

No late work will be accepted, but I am very amenable to offering extensions. If you think you might need an extension or accommodation for any reason, please let me know.

At the very least, students must submit both papers and complete the final exam to receive a passing grade – failure to submit a paper or complete the exam will result in an automatic F.

Netiquette Guidelines

  • Act professionally in the way you communicate. Treat your instructors and peers with respect, the same way you would do in a face-to-face environment. Respect other people’s ideas and be constructive when explaining your views about points you may not agree with. 
  • Be sensitive. Be respectful and sensitive when sharing your ideas and opinions. There will be people in your class with different linguistic backgrounds, political and religious beliefs or other general differences. 
  • Proofread and check spelling. Doing this before sending an email or posting a thread on a discussion board will allow you to make sure your message is clear and thoughtful. Avoid the use of all capital letters, it can be perceived as if you are shouting, and it is more difficult to read. 
  • Keep your communications focused and stay on topic. Complete your ideas before changing the subject. By keeping the message on focus you allow the readers to easily get your idea or answers they are looking for. 
  • Be clear with your message. Avoid using humor or sarcasm. Since people can’t see your expressions or hear your tone of voice, meaning can be misinterpreted.
