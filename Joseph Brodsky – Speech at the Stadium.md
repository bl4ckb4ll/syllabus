# Joseph Brodsky — “Speech at the Stadium”

Commencement address delivered at the University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, in December 1988 and collected in *On Grief and Reason: Essays*.

- **Author:** Joseph Brodsky
- **Occasion:** University of Michigan commencement, Ann Arbor, 1988
- **Collection:** *On Grief and Reason: Essays*
- **Book record:** https://books.google.com/books/about/On_Grief_and_Reason.html?id=LLKDfyQF5EUC
- **Readable transcript / discussion:** https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/12/18/joseph-brodsky-speech-at-the-stadium-commencement/

## Why it belongs

Brodsky addresses young people at the point when advice about education, work, success, and adulthood is supposed to become useful. His subject is larger than professional success. He asks what experience can actually pass from one generation to another, and offers practical moral advice for living in a world whose rules are learned partly by watching what people do.

For Black Ball, the essay belongs with questions about consequential advice to young people. Good advice should not merely improve the odds of earning money or avoiding poverty. It should also help a person avoid being drawn into habits, institutions, incentives, and forms of conduct that he may later regret having accepted as normal.

That makes the essay useful beside material about education and careers. A career can succeed economically while changing what a person tolerates, rewards, ignores, or learns not to see. Brodsky's address gives a vocabulary for asking what kind of person a supposedly successful path is training someone to become.

The opening is also unusually direct about moral complexity: life has rules but no referee, people learn by watching, many play dirty, and inherited moral instruction does not automatically determine what people later do with it. That is part of why this works as a reading for young people rather than merely as retrospective moral judgment.

## Personal note

I have read the opening sentences to my children because I want them to understand early that the world is not run only by nice teachers. The point is not to frighten them. It is to introduce moral complexity before adulthood makes the lesson expensive.

The benefit of good advice — and even of learning from bad advice — should not be measured only by whether it keeps a young person from becoming poor. Advice can also help someone avoid getting sucked into behavior that, years later, he recognizes with regret. Some people may never make that recognition. Plenty of others do.

A related developer-log note should explore this problem in the recent software boom: what happens when a field briefly acquires money, prestige, and cultural permission, and how quickly those rewards can make conduct look normal that would be easier to recognize from historical distance.
