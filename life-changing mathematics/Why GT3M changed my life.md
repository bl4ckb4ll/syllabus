# Why GT3M changed my life

> **AI-written stub.** This is not the user’s own prose; it is a placeholder for later revision.

There are at least two different audiences for this question. The first has little idea what mathematics is beyond school arithmetic. The second is harder: it already has a theory of mathematics, but the theory is wrong. Ignorance leaves room; a misconception occupies the room.

## 1. For people who have no idea what mathematics is

William Thurston’s *The Geometry and Topology of Three-Manifolds* (GT3M) is difficult to explain as “a textbook,” because much of its force comes from showing mathematics before it has been embalmed into definitions, lemmas, and proofs. It feels more like watching an extraordinarily powerful mathematician invent a way of seeing a subject while you are standing next to him.

Three-dimensional topology becomes a world of objects that can bend, stretch, decompose, acquire hyperbolic geometry, and reveal global structure. Pictures are not decoration or an embarrassing preliminary to the real proof; they are part of the reasoning. The notes leave unfinished edges—questions, constructions, guesses, directions—and therefore make mathematics look like something a living person can enter rather than a museum of completed results.

GT3M also makes boundaries between subjects feel less real. Topology, geometry, group theory, dynamics, and complex analysis begin to look like different views of the same objects. It quietly changes the standard for what it means to understand something: knowing that a theorem is true is not enough; you want to know what the space looks like, why the phenomenon should happen, how it deforms, and what it is connected to.

So “GT3M changed my life” does not mean merely “this was my favorite textbook.” It means something closer to: **this book changed my conception of what mathematical thought could be, and therefore changed what seemed worth spending a life doing.**

## 2. For people who think they know what mathematics is

This audience can be harder to reach because it may already have an instrumental picture of mathematics. Mathematics is a stockpile of difficult techniques: learn analysis, statistics, optimization, coding, and probability; become more employable, become a quant, build software, obtain prestige, predict something useful. Every subject is then interrogated with the same question: **but is it applied?**

There is nothing wrong with applications or with earning a living. The mistake is treating external usefulness as the permission slip that makes mathematics worth doing.

Even consuming elite mathematics courses can fit the same misconception. Watching a famous analysis course can become a form of self-improvement: accumulate harder material, acquire increasingly prestigious skills, become a more formidable person. That is still different from mathematical attachment.

GT3M pushes in another direction. It keeps saying, in effect: look at this object. What can it possibly be doing? What happens if we cut it here, deform it there, or put a geometric structure on it? The reason to continue is often simply that the next question has become impossible to leave alone.

That is the distinction between **mastery** and **attachment**. Mastery asks, “How much mathematics can I learn?” Attachment eventually says, “I need to understand this.”

Once that transition happens, applications do not become bad or irrelevant. They simply stop being necessary as justification. **The subject stops needing an application because the question itself has become a reason to care.**

That is one way GT3M can permanently alter a life: not by supplying a more valuable technique, but by changing the reason one does mathematics at all.
